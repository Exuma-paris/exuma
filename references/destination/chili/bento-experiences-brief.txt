Subject: the night sky over the Chilean altiplano, above the high desert near San Pedro de Atacama. A landscape photograph — no people, no equipment, no telescope.

The sky: this is the subject and it must fill most of the frame. The southern Milky Way running diagonally across it, dense, structured and grainy with stars, its dark dust lanes clearly readable. The two Magellanic Clouds hanging apart from it as faint smudges of light. Thousands of stars of different magnitudes and colours — cold white, blue-white, a few amber — never a uniform sprinkle. The transparency of very high, very dry air: no light pollution, no glow on the horizon except a last trace of deep blue where the sun went down.

The land: a wide altiplano basin at over four thousand metres. The dark cone of a volcano stands on the horizon, symmetrical, snow in its upper gullies catching the starlight. In front of it, a shallow high-altitude lagoon lies perfectly still and mirrors the sky, its salt-crusted margin a pale ring in the darkness. Low tufts of coarse altiplano grass and scattered volcanic stones in the near foreground, the ground pale and cracked.

Light: starlight only, plus the faintest cool afterglow low on the horizon. The land is a dark silhouette with just enough detail to read its texture; the sky carries all the luminosity. Deep desaturated blues and cold greys against warm dark earth tones. Blacks stay black — dense, never lifted, never milky, never a bright blue night.

Camera: eye level, standing on the shore of the lagoon — never aerial. Wide angle, around 24 mm, a long exposure on a fixed tripod: stars are points, not trails. Everything in focus from the foreground stones to the volcano. Fine 35 mm film grain consistent with a high-ISO night frame.

Realism: an astrophotograph made on location, not a composite and not a fantasy sky — plausible star density, plausible Milky Way structure, no exaggerated nebula colours, no aurora, no shooting stars, no oversaturated purple.
