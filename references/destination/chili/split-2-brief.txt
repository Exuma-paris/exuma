Subject: the barrel cellar of a family estate in the Colchagua valley, Chile, where the carménère ages. An empty room — NO PEOPLE anywhere in the frame, no hands, no silhouette, no figure in the distance. The photograph is about the barrels themselves.

The barrels: French oak barriques on low black steel racks, stacked two high, in two long rows that run away from the camera and converge in the depth of the room. Every barrel head tells of use — dried purple-black wine drips running down from the bung, a chalk or wax-crayon lot number and a vintage scrawled by hand on the wood, iron hoops dulled and lightly rusted, staves raised and greyed with age, one bung set slightly askew. The wood is not uniform: some barrels newer and pale, others darkened by years. On one head, a glass wine thief and a single stemmed tasting glass have been set down and left there, a little wine still in the bottom.

The room: a genuine working cellar, not a showroom. Rough plastered walls, a low vaulted or beamed ceiling lost in shadow, a bare polished-concrete floor still damp from the morning wash with a shallow puddle catching the light. Along the wall, a coiled hose, a stainless bucket, a wooden pallet, a wall-mounted thermometer.

Light: low, warm and directional. A wide shaft of late-afternoon daylight enters from an open doorway at the far end of the aisle and lays a long bright band down the wet floor, grazing the nearest barrel heads and picking out the raised grain of the oak. Two weak tungsten bulbs hang between the rows, warm and dim. The depth of the room falls into deep, dense shadow. Palette of warm ochre, oak brown and garnet; blacks stay black, never lifted or milky, never an evenly lit modern winery.

Camera: eye level, standing in the middle of the aisle — never aerial. Around 35 to 50 mm, natural perspective, symmetry of the two rows leading the eye to the lit doorway. Deep enough focus that the near barrel heads and their chalk marks are sharp and legible, the far end softer. Visible 35 mm film grain in the shadows.

Realism: an ordinary, slightly imperfect reportage frame — dust, drips and clutter left where they fall, nothing arranged for the camera.
